Appendix L: Learning outcomes

This appendix states what a reader can do after each part of the book, and names the chapters that carry the evidence for each outcome.

The outcomes are written as things a reader can do, because that is what the chapters ask for and what the portfolioThe README of your repository and the files it links to, at a named tag. in Chapter 12 shows. Each chapter's "What to record" names the outcomes its record evidences. Three chapters have the reader study a finished state of the code and check it rather than build it, outcomes 5, 8, 9 and 10, and the outcomes say so.

A module adopting the book maps them to its own outcome codes on the institution page (Chapter 15); the book itself carries none.

L.1 Foundations

After Chapter 1 and Chapter 2, you can:

  1. Explain why a page rendered on the server that works with JavaScript off is the baseline, and what HTMXA JavaScript library that lets HTML attributes such as hx-get, hx-post, hx-target and hx-swap make requests and swap the returned HTML into the page. adds to it and what it does not.
  2. Run a short needs-findingFinding out what people are trying to do and what stops them, by asking and watching, before deciding what to build. interview with spoken consentA participant’s agreement to take part after being told what will be recorded, kept and deleted, and that they can stop at any time., keep notes in which the quotation and your reading of it are distinct, and write a need as a job storyA need written as a situation, a motivation, an outcome and the constraint behind them: “When …, I want to …, so I can …, because …”..
  3. Build a backlogThe CSV file of things to fix, one row each, with the evidence that justifies the row. in which every row points at its evidence, and choose what to fix by severityThe backlog’s three-value column; its values are in @sec-needs-finding-and-consent. and by who is excluded.
  4. Collect the minimum of personal dataAnything that identifies a living person, alone or with other information: a name, an email address, a student number, a face, a voice., say where it is kept and when it is deleted, and keep to that.

L.2 Building inclusively

After Chapter 3 to Chapter 6, you can:

  1. Read an interaction that works by mouse, by keyboard and with a screen readerSoftware that reads the accessibility tree aloud or to a braille display and lets a person navigate it from the keyboard., in both the enhanced and the plain pathThe way an interaction works with JavaScript off: a plain form submission, a server response and a full page., from one templateA file of HTML with placeholders that the server fills from a map of values before sending it; the reference code’s templates are Pebble files under templates/., check it in all three ways, and explain how its templates and routesA method and a path the server answers, with the handler that answers it: get("/tasks") in TaskRoutes.kt is one. achieve it.
  2. AuditA review of a page against a standard, here WCAG 2.2, recording each defect against the success criterion it fails. a page against WCAGThe Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, version 2.2, the W3C recommendation the book audits against. 2.2 with an automated scan, a keyboard, a screen reader and a contrast check, and record each defect against its success criterionOne testable statement in WCAG, numbered like 2.1.1, with a level..
  3. Fix a defect, show the diffThe lines that differ between two versions of the files, as git diff prints them: a line removed, a line added., and show that the fix passes the check that found it.
  4. Explain how templates factored into partialsA template that renders one part of a page, named with a leading underscore, included by the page template and rendered on its own for a fragment. let a fragmentA piece of HTML without the page around it, returned to an HTMX request and swapped into the page. and a page come from one source, and check that every view has a URL that works without JavaScript.
  5. Read how input is validated on the server and the error returned in both paths, check every flow in both, and state the trade-offs of keeping parityThe requirement that every task that can be completed with JavaScript on can be completed with it off, ending in the same state..

L.3 Evaluating

After Chapter 7 to Chapter 10, you can:

  1. Write an evaluation plan with tasks and metrics, and read and check an instrumented server that logs one row per request without recording anything that identifies a participantA person who takes part in an interview or a pilot..
  2. Run a pilotA session in which a participant attempts set tasks on the instrumented application while you take notes. with consenting participants, debriefThe conversation with a participant immediately after a pilot session, in which they say what was hard and why. them, and keep notes from which a reader can trace a findingA paragraph in research/findings.md making a claim about what happened in the pilots, with its source: “participants without JavaScript failed the edit task in two of four saves”, with the rows that show it. to its source.
  3. Compute the medianThe middle value when the values are sorted, with half above it and half below., the median absolute deviation and the error rateThe proportion of requests for a task that ended in an error, or of saves for the edit task. for each task in each mode, and turn a difference into a finding with its source.
  4. Rank findings by their score and the cost of a change, and say why the top one is top.
  5. Make a change, run the regression checksA check run after a change to confirm that something that worked still works. before and after it, re-pilotA pilot run after a change, with the same tasks, to see whether the measurement moved., and assemble the chain from finding to verification, reporting a change that did not help as plainly as one that did.

L.4 Communicating

After Chapter 11 and Chapter 12, you can:

  1. Present one evidence chainThe four links from a finding to the measurement that showed it, the change that addressed it and the verification that the change worked, as @sec-redesign-and-verification names them. in five minutes, answer questions from the files rather than from memory, and respond to someone else's work by naming what you saw and what you could not see.
  2. Write a portfolio in which every claim links to the data, code or screenshot behind it, state its limitations, and reflect in prose on what the evidence does not show.
