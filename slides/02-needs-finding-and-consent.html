Needs-finding and consent

Server-First HCI

What you will have built

  • A consent process you can run
  • Notes from your first interviews
  • Five or six job stories
  • A backlog in which every row points at the evidence for it

Nothing in this session touches the code. You interview one other person, write the needs you heard as job stories, and turn them into the backlog that every later chapter adds rows to. The consent process comes first, because the notes you take are personal data however small the interview is.

Before you start

  • Read the privacy and consent appendix; it holds the principles this chapter relies on
  • Have the consent form from the appendices to hand
  • One other person to interview and about two hours
  • Nothing in this chapter touches the code

The appendix on privacy and consent is the one piece of reading, and the consent form is the one document you need printed or open. Pair up before the session starts so that the interview can begin without hunting for a partner. The code stays as you left it after the last chapter.

A requirement names a solution; a need names a situation

  • "The system shall allow users to filter tasks by status"
  • "When I am preparing for a review, I want to see only the tasks I have to hand in, so I can check I have not missed one"
  • Solutions date and needs do not
  • Know the need, and you can change the solution when the evidence changes

Put the two sentences side by side. The first names a feature and says nothing about who wanted it or when; the second names a moment, a motivation and an outcome. If you know the need you can change the solution when the evidence or the technology changes, and you can tell when a solution has not worked, which a requirement never tells you.

The job story

When [situation],
I want to [motivation],
so I can [outcome],
because [constraint].

The job story is the form this book uses to write a need down, taken from Klement's short article, which is in the further reading. It has four parts, and the fourth is the one people leave out: the constraint is what makes the motivation make sense. Every story in your stories file has all four lines.

One from the application you built

When I am checking my task list with a screen reader,
I want the confirmation announced as soon as I delete a task,
so I can know it worked without going back to the list,
because re-reading twenty items to find one gap takes minutes.

This is the gap you recorded at the end of the last chapter, written as a story. The situation is specific about the moment, and the constraint says why the announcement matters in a way a reader can check. Compare it with the persona form on the next slide.

Why not a persona

  • "As a blind user, I want screen reader support so I can use the app"
  • A label where the situation should be
  • Assumes everyone under the label needs the same thing
  • Says nothing about why
  • Someone whose trackpad broke this morning is, for today, navigating by keyboard

The persona form puts a label where the situation should be, assumes everyone under the label needs the same thing, and says nothing about why. The job story is specific about the moment, and that specificity covers people the label leaves out. The story about keyboard access belongs to the person whose trackpad broke this morning as much as to anyone.

Consent first

  • What another person says about their working life is personal data
  • Collect the minimum: no names, student numbers or email addresses
  • A pseudonym such as P1 in their place
  • Say where the notes will be kept and when they will be deleted, and keep to it
  • The participant can stop, see their notes, or have them deleted; tell them how

Three of the principles from the appendix bite here. Collect the minimum, say what will happen to the data and keep to it, and make the participant's rights real by telling them how to use them. The pseudonym replaces the name from the first line of the notes, not later.

Spoken consent is enough here

  • A short interview between classmates, no recording, no identifying data
  • Copy the consent form into your repository as research/consent-form.md
  • Fill the contact fields from your institution's page
  • Read it to your participant or hand it to them
  • Record the date, the pseudonym, and that consent was given; nothing else

For this exercise spoken consent is enough, provided you have said what you will do before you start. The consent form is the record of what you said, and the only things written about the participant are the date, the pseudonym you assigned and the fact of consent. The fuller participant information sheet is for the pilots, where you will be timing people; it is more than this needs.

A name in the history

  • If you find yourself typing a participant's name into the notes to tidy up later, stop
  • Replace it now
  • A name committed to a git history is a name you cannot promise to delete

This is the one rule that cannot be fixed afterwards. Deleting a file does not remove its contents from the history, so a name that reaches a commit stays in the repository, and the promise you made on the consent form is broken. Replace the name with the pseudonym before you save.

Interview your partner

  • Ten minutes each, then swap; a third person if there is time
  • Six prompts, in your own words; let the answer run
  • "Can you tell me more about that?", "what did you do instead?", "how did that feel?"
  • When they describe a feature, ask what they were doing when they wanted it
  • When nothing frustrates them, ask about the last deadline they nearly missed

The six prompts are in the chapter and run from the last time they used a task list to the one thing they would change. Use them in your own words and follow the answer rather than the list. A feature a participant asks for is their solution; the situation underneath it is what you want, so ask what they were doing when they wanted it.

The notes

  • research/notes.md, one heading per participant, one subheading per prompt
  • What they said, as close to their words as you can manage
  • What you noticed: hesitation, a workaround they described
  • One or two theme tags from a short vocabulary agreed beforehand
  • Keep the quotation and your reading of it visibly distinct

Under each prompt write three things, and keep them apart on the page. The quotation is evidence; your reading of it is a claim about the evidence, and the backlog treats the two differently. Agree the tag vocabulary with your partner before you start, so that the same thing gets the same tag in both sets of notes.

Write job stories

  • Read both sets of notes together; list each situation where someone struggled
  • Five or six stories in research/stories.md, numbered S1 onward
  • Each: the four lines, the evidence (participant and line number), who else the situation catches
  • The WCAG 2.2 success criterion it maps to, where there is one
  • The same thing from both participants is one story with two pieces of evidence

A story carries its evidence with it: the participant and the line in the notes it came from. It also names who else the situation catches, such as a person using a screen reader or someone under cognitive load, and the criterion it maps to where there is one; the criteria the book uses are listed in the auditing chapter. A story from your own testing, such as the silent redirect, is written too, with the evidence recorded as your own observation.

Build the backlog

  • backlog/backlog.csv; its header is defined in the chapter and nowhere else
  • Later chapters add rows and never change the columns
  • story_type is job story, observation or audit
  • Aim for eight rows, mixing the interviews with what you noticed building the list
  • A row with an empty evidence cell is an opinion

The chapter holds the header and a table saying what each column holds, and that page owns them; this slide does not restate them. Eight rows is enough to score and choose from. A row with no evidence can stay in the file, but it does not become a candidate fix.

Choose the candidate fixes

  • Severity scores three for high, two for medium, one for low
  • Add one if more than one group is caught
  • Add two for a Level A failure, one for Level AA
  • Mark one or two of the highest-scoring rows candidate_fix=true
  • Only if each can be fixed in an hour or two and has a criterion to check it against

The worked row in the chapter scores five: high severity, two groups caught, and a criterion at AA. A cosmetic row that catches one group and maps to no criterion scores one. The analysis chapter adds the cost of a fix to this scoring once you have measured something; until then severity and inclusion are enough to choose by.

What goes wrong

  • Two stories for one need, because both participants said the same thing
  • A story with no evidence marked as a candidate fix
  • A feature request written down as if it were a need
  • A name in the notes, kept to tidy up later

The first three are caught when you read the stories back against the notes; the fourth is caught only before you save. If both participants said the same thing, that is one story with two pieces of evidence, and the second piece makes it stronger, not a second row. A row whose evidence cell is empty can stay, but it is not a candidate.

What to record

  • Commit research/consent-form.md, research/notes.md, research/stories.md and backlog/backlog.csv
  • Check that no file contains a name, a student number or an email address other than your own
  • Note, at the end of the stories file, which stories claim time or errors; those are the ones you measure later
  • Trace one row from the notes to the story to the backlog

Four files, and a check for personal data before the commit. The stories that make a claim about time or errors, such as how long it takes to confirm a deletion, are the ones the instrumentation chapter measures, so mark them now in a sentence. Then pick one row and trace it back to the line in the notes; whatever you cannot trace is what the evidence chain will need before the assessment.
