Appendix L: Learning outcomes

This appendix states what a reader can do after each part of the book, and names the chapters that carry the evidence for each outcome.

The outcomes are written as things a reader can do, because that is what the chapters ask for and what the portfolio in Chapter 12 shows.

A module adopting the book maps them to its own outcome codes on its institution page; the book itself carries none.

L.1 Foundations

After Chapter 1 and Chapter 2, you can:

  1. Explain why a page rendered on the server that works with JavaScript off is the baseline, and what HTMX adds to it and what it does not.
  2. Run a short needs-finding interview with spoken consent, keep notes in which the quotation and your reading of it are distinct, and write a need as a job story.
  3. Build a backlog in which every row points at its evidence, and choose what to fix by severity and by who is excluded.
  4. Collect the minimum of personal data, say where it is kept and when it is deleted, and keep to that.

L.2 Building inclusively

After Chapter 3 to Chapter 6, you can:

  1. Build an interaction that works by mouse, by keyboard and with a screen reader, in both the enhanced and the plain path, from one template.
  2. Audit a page against WCAG 2.2 with an automated scan, a keyboard, a screen reader and a contrast check, and record each defect against its success criterion.
  3. Fix a defect, show the diff, and show that the fix passes the check that found it.
  4. Factor templates into partials so that a fragment and a page come from the same source, and give every view a URL that works without JavaScript.
  5. Validate input on the server, return the error in both paths, and state the trade-offs of keeping parity.

L.3 Evaluating

After Chapter 7 to Chapter 10, you can:

  1. Write an evaluation plan with tasks and metrics, and instrument a server to log one row per event without recording anything that identifies a participant.
  2. Run a pilot with consenting participants, debrief them, and keep notes from which a reader can trace a finding to its source.
  3. Compute the median, the median absolute deviation and the error rate for each task in each condition, and turn a difference into a finding with its source.
  4. Rank findings by impact, inclusion and effort, and say why the top one is top.
  5. Make a change, run the regression checks, re-pilot, and assemble the chain from data to verification, reporting a change that did not help as plainly as one that did.

L.4 Communicating

After Chapter 11 and Chapter 12, you can:

  1. Present one evidence chain in five minutes, answer questions from the files rather than from memory, and respond to someone else's work by naming what you saw and what you could not see.
  2. Write a portfolio in which every claim links to the data, code or screenshot behind it, state its limitations, and reflect in prose on what the evidence does not show.
