Appendix E: Accessibility testing

This appendix is the method behind the audit in Chapter 4 and the regression checks in Chapter 10: four tests of ten minutes each, what each can and cannot find, and a checklist to run them from.

No single test finds everything. Deque's study of its own audit data found that automated rules detected 57 per cent of the issues found across the pages in its sample (Deque Systems, 2021); the rest were found by a person trying to use the page. The four parts below are ordered so that each catches what the one before cannot: the scan finds the mechanical faults, the keyboard finds what the scan cannot see, the screen reader finds what the keyboard cannot hear, and the contrast check measures what none of the other three do. Run all four on each state of the page that matters: the list, the edit form, and the page after a failed submit.

E.1 The automated scan

Open the page, open DevTools, choose the axe DevTools tab and scan the whole page; installing the extension is in Appendix A. Each result names the rule that fired, the element it fired on and the WCAG success criterion it maps to (W3C, 2023), with Deque's severity words critical, serious, moderate and minor, which are the tool's vocabulary and not the book's. Record the rule, the element and the criterion for each.

The scan is reliable on what can be decided from the markup alone: a form control without a label, an image without alt, text whose colour fails the ratio against a plain background, a missing lang attribute, duplicate id values, a heading sequence that skips a level. It cannot decide whether a label makes sense, where focus goes after a swap, whether a status message is announced, whether the page can be operated without a mouse, or whether anything works with JavaScript off. A scan with no violations is where the audit starts, not where it ends.

Scan the page in each of its states. The scan sees the document as it is at that moment; an error message that exists only after a failed submit is scanned only if you fail a submit first.

E.2 Keyboard only

Put the mouse out of reach. Click once in the address bar and press Tab; the first stop should be the skip link, and Enter on it should move focus into the main content. Keep pressing Tab, and at each stop ask whether you can see where you are and whether the control does what it says with Enter or Space. Shift-Tab goes back. Complete every task the application supports, adding, editing, marking done, deleting, filtering and paging, without touching the mouse.

This finds a control that cannot be reached, which usually means a clickable element that is not a button or a link; a stop with no visible focus ring (2.4.7); an order that jumps about the page (2.4.3); a trap that focus can enter and not leave (2.1.2); and the one particular to this book, focus falling to the top of the document after an HTMX swap, so that the next Tab starts again from the skip link. It cannot tell you what a screen reader would announce, and it cannot measure contrast; a faint focus ring is a contrast question, below.

Record the order of the stops and any stop that failed, as a sentence or a short list; a screenshot of a focused control shows the ring. Ten minutes covers the application once; where a chapter changed a template, Tab through that state again.

E.3 Screen reader

Use the screen reader for your platform, installed as Appendix A says. Three keys do most of the work in any of them: Tab moves focus between controls, H moves to the next heading, and one key switches between reading the page and operating a control.

NVDA: start it with Ctrl-Alt-N. Its modifier key is Insert, or Caps Lock if you chose that when installing. NVDA-Space switches between browse mode, in which H and F jump to the next heading and form field, and focus mode, in which keystrokes go to the control. Tools, Speech Viewer in the NVDA menu opens a window that logs everything spoken; copy from it for a transcript.

VoiceOver: Cmd-F5 toggles it; use it in Safari. Its modifier is Ctrl-Option, written VO. VO-Right Arrow reads the next item, VO-U opens the rotor, which lists headings, form controls and landmarks, and Tab moves focus as usual.

Orca: Super-Alt-S toggles it on GNOME. Its modifier is Insert on a desktop keyboard layout and Caps Lock on a laptop layout. Orca Modifier-A switches between browse mode and focus mode, and H moves to the next heading in browse mode. Orca has no speech log; write down what you hear as it is said.

With the reader running, do three things on the task list. Move through the headings and landmarks and listen for the structure. Tab to each control and listen for its name and role: "Edit, button" tells you the label works, and "button" alone tells you it does not. Submit the add form with an empty title and listen for the error, then with a valid title and listen for the status message; a live region that works is announced without your focus moving (4.1.3), and an error linked to its field with aria-describedby is read when you Tab back to the field (3.3.1).

This finds a control with no accessible name, a status message that is never announced, an error with no link to its field, an image whose alt text is its file name, and a reading order that differs from the visual one. It cannot find a faint focus ring, a low-contrast label, or anything about what a sighted person navigating by keyboard sees. Record what was announced, as text; a Speech Viewer log is the strongest form of this evidence, and a sentence of your own is acceptable where the tool has none.

E.4 Contrast and zoom

Text needs a ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background, large text 3:1, and controls and focus indicators 3:1 against what surrounds them (1.4.3 and 1.4.11). In Chrome, inspect the element and click the colour swatch in the Styles pane: the picker shows the ratio and whether it meets AA. In Firefox, the Accessibility panel's "Check for issues" menu has a contrast option that lists every failing element on the page. For a pair of values taken from a stylesheet, the WebAIM contrast checker takes two hex colours and gives the ratio (WebAIM, no date).

Then zoom to 200 per cent with Ctrl and plus until the browser says so, and narrow the window to about 320 CSS pixels (the device toolbar in Chrome DevTools, or Ctrl-Shift-M in Firefox for Responsive Design Mode). Nothing should be cut off and there should be no horizontal scrolling (1.4.10).

This finds grey placeholder text standing in for a label, a focus ring one shade from its background, and a layout that clips at zoom. It cannot find information carried by colour alone (1.4.1), which needs a person to notice that "red means overdue" has no other signal, and it guesses at the background when text sits over an image or a gradient.

E.5 The checklist

Run from this table, the four parts in order; the last column says what to keep. Where a row is also one of the regression checks in Chapter 10, that chapter's checklist is the one the assessment reads; this one is for the audit and for the end of any lab that changed a template.

What makes a screenshot count as evidence, and how to name and store it, is in Appendix J. A failed check becomes a backlog row of the audit kind, with the criterion in its evidence column, as Chapter 2 defines.

E.6 Further reading

The W3C's Easy Checks walks through a first review of a page with nothing beyond a browser (W3C Web Accessibility Initiative, no date). The WCAG 2.2 quick reference is where a criterion number is looked up (W3C, 2023).
