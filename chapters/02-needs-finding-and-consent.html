2  Needs-finding and consent

By the end you have a consent process you can run, notes from your first interviews, five or six job stories, and a backlog in which every row points at the evidence for it.

Before you start, read Appendix F, which holds the data-protection principles this chapter relies on, and have the consent form from Appendix H to hand. You need one other person to interview and about two hours. Nothing in this chapter touches the code.

2.1 Why ask people

A requirement names a solution: "the system shall allow users to filter tasks by status". A need names a situation and what the person in it is trying to do: "when I am preparing for a review, I want to see only the tasks I have to hand in, so I can check I have not missed one". Solutions date and needs do not. If you know the need, you can change the solution when the evidence or the technology changes, and you can tell when a solution has not worked.

The job story is the form this book uses to write a need down (Klement, 2013). It has four parts: a situation, a motivation, an outcome, and the constraint behind them.

When [situation],
I want to [motivation],
so I can [outcome],
because [constraint].

An example from the application you built in Chapter 1:

When I am checking my task list with a screen reader,
I want the confirmation announced as soon as I delete a task,
so I can know it worked without going back to the list,
because re-reading twenty items to find one gap takes minutes.

Compare the persona form: "As a blind user, I want screen reader support so I can use the app." It puts a label where the situation should be, assumes everyone under the label needs the same thing, and says nothing about why. The job story is specific about the moment, and that specificity covers people the label leaves out. Someone whose trackpad broke this morning is, for today, navigating by keyboard, and the story about keyboard access is theirs as well.

2.2 Consent first

You are about to write down what another person says about their working life. That is personal data, however small the interview, and the rules in Appendix F apply. Three of them bite here. Collect the minimum: no names, no student numbers, no email addresses; a pseudonym such as P1 in their place. Say where the notes will be kept and when they will be deleted, and keep to it. Tell the participant they can stop at any point, see their notes, or have them deleted, and tell them how to ask.

For a short interview between classmates, with no recording and no identifying data, spoken consent is enough, provided you have said what you will do before you start. Copy the consent form in Appendix H into your repository as research/consent-form.md, fill the contact fields in from your institution's page, and read it to your participant or hand it to them. Record the date, the pseudonym you assigned, and that consent was given. Nothing else about the participant goes in the file. The fuller participant information sheet in Appendix G is for the pilots in Chapter 8, where you will be timing people; it is more than this exercise needs.

If you find yourself typing a participant's name into the notes to tidy up later, stop and replace it now. A name committed to a git history is a name you cannot promise to delete.

2.3 Activity 1: interview your partner

Pair up. Each of you interviews the other for ten minutes, then you swap. If there is time, find a third person. Use these prompts, in your own words, and let the answer run before you move on.

  1. Tell me about the last time you used a to-do list or task manager. What were you trying to get done?
  2. What frustrates you most about keeping track of tasks? Can you give me a specific time it happened?
  3. Have you ever lost track of something important? What happened?
  4. Do you ever work without a mouse or trackpad, on a train, or when something has broken? How does that change how you use things?
  5. Have you tried to use something like this with your eyes closed, or in bright sunlight? What was hard?
  6. If you could change one thing to make this less stressful, what would it be?

When an answer is short, ask "can you tell me more about that?", "what did you do instead?", or "how did that feel?". When the participant describes a feature they would like, ask what they were doing when they wanted it; the feature is their solution, and you want the situation underneath. When they say nothing frustrates them, ask about the last deadline they nearly missed.

Keep the notes in research/notes.md, one heading per participant and one subheading per prompt. Under each prompt write three things: what they said, as close to their words as you can manage; what you noticed, including hesitation or a workaround they described; and one or two theme tags from a short vocabulary you agree with your partner beforehand, such as confirmation, keyboard, contrast, filter, progress. Keep a quotation and your reading of it visibly distinct. The quotation is evidence; your reading is a claim about it.

2.4 Activity 2: write job stories

Read both sets of notes together. List each situation in which someone struggled, the need behind it, and where in the notes it came from. Then write five or six job stories in research/stories.md, numbered S1 onward. Each story has the four lines above, the evidence (participant and line number in the notes), who else the situation catches (a person using a screen reader, navigating by keyboard, with low vision, under cognitive load, or with a motor impairment), and the WCAG 2.2 success criterion it maps to where there is one (W3C, 2023). The criteria this book uses, by number and level, are listed in Chapter 4; look ahead to it now.

Two things go wrong at this step. If both participants said the same thing, that is one story with two pieces of evidence, not two stories. If a story comes from your own testing rather than an interview, such as the no-JavaScript path giving no feedback after a redirect, write it anyway and record the evidence as your own observation. The backlog treats the two kinds of evidence differently, so the distinction has to survive.

2.5 Activity 3: build the backlog

The backlog is a CSV file at backlog/backlog.csv. Its header is defined here and nowhere else; later chapters add rows to it and never change the columns.

id,title,story_ref,story_type,need,type,severity,inclusion_risk,evidence,notes,candidate_fix
Column Holds
id A number, unique within the file.
title Under ten words.
story_ref stories.md#S2, or notes.md#L15 for an item straight from the notes.
story_type job story, observation, or audit for an item that will come from Chapter 4.
need The need in a phrase: "confirmation", "keyboard access".
type accessibility, usability, or technical.
severity high blocks the task or excludes a group; medium makes it harder but a workaround exists; low is cosmetic.
inclusion_risk Who is caught, comma-separated: screen reader, keyboard, low vision, cognitive, motor.
evidence The notes reference, a WCAG criterion number, or own observation.
notes Anything that explains the row.
candidate_fix true for the one or two rows you will fix in Chapter 4.

Aim for eight rows, mixing stories from the interviews with what you noticed building the application in Chapter 1. One row in full, to set the pattern:

2,No confirmation after submitting the add form without JavaScript,stories.md#S2,job story,confirmation,accessibility,high,"cognitive,screen reader",P1 notes L12; P2 notes L8,The HTMX path announces a status; the redirect path says nothing,true

To choose the candidate fixes, score each row. Severity scores three for high, two for medium, one for low. Add one if more than one group is caught. Add two if the row is a Level A failure and one if it is Level AA. The row above scores five: high severity, two groups, and 4.1.3 at AA. A cosmetic row that catches one group and maps to no criterion scores one. Mark one or two of the highest-scoring rows candidate_fix=true, provided each can be fixed in an hour or two and has a criterion to check it against afterwards. Chapter 9 adds the cost of a fix to this scoring once you have measured something; until then, severity and inclusion are enough to choose by.

A row with an empty evidence cell is an opinion. Keep it if you want to, but it does not become a candidate fix.

2.6 What to record

Commit research/consent-form.md, research/notes.md, research/stories.md and backlog/backlog.csv. Check that no file contains a name, a student number or an email address other than your own. The stories that make a claim about time or errors, such as how long it takes to confirm a deletion, are the ones Chapter 7 measures; note which they are, in a sentence at the end of the stories file.

For your own notes, two questions. Look at your backlog and consider how the rows would read if you had written requirements instead of stories. Pick one row and trace it from the notes to the story to the backlog; whatever you cannot trace is what the evidence chain will need before the assessment.

2.7 Further reading

Klement's article introducing job stories is short and worth reading in full (Klement, 2013). The ICO's guide to UK GDPR is the source for the principles in Appendix F. The WCAG 2.2 quick reference is the fastest way to look up a criterion by number (W3C, 2023).
