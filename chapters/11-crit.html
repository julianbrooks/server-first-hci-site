11  Crit

By the end you have presented one evidence chain to a group of peers in five minutes, answered their questions from the files rather than from memory, written a response to each of theirs, and recorded the questions you could not answer.

Before the lab, finish the evidence chain from Chapter 10, have the application running at the tag that holds your fix, and bring the before and after screenshots named as Appendix J describes. Nothing in this chapter changes the code.

11.1 What a crit is for

A crit is a conversation about one piece of work with the evidence in the room. You put the chain in front of people who were not there when you built it, and they find the step that only holds together because you remember it doing so. That step is worth finding now, while there is a week to fix it, rather than when the marker reaches it.

The chain has four links, and you present them in order: the finding, the measurement that showed it, the fix, and the verification that the fix worked. Each link has a file behind it. The finding is a backlog row and the notes it points at; the measurement is rows of the metrics file and the figures Chapter 9 computed from them; the fix is a commit; the verification is the regression checklist and the re-pilot from Chapter 10. If one link has no file, you have found the gap before anyone else does.

The audience responds to the evidence, not the person. "The focus ring is faint" is about the work; "you did not test it properly" is about you, and the second kind is not permitted here. The useful question asks to see something: which rows of the metrics file, which line of the notes, what the no-JS path did at the same point, what the screen reader said. A question that cannot be answered from a file is still useful, because it tells the presenter what the portfolio is missing.

11.2 Activity 1: prepare the chain

Choose the chain you will present. If you fixed more than one thing, choose the fix with the strongest measurement behind it, not the one you are proudest of. Write crit/outline.md with four headings, finding, measurement, fix and verification, and under each the sentence you will say and the path of the file you will show. The sentence names the thing and the figure: "participants without JavaScript failed the edit task in two of six attempts", not "the edit task had problems". Keep the file under a page; it is a prompt sheet, not a script.

Decide what you will show live and what you will show as a screenshot. Showing the fixed behaviour live is stronger than a picture of it, because the picture could be from any commit. Showing the broken behaviour live means checking out the earlier tag during your five minutes, which is time you do not have; the before screenshot carries that link. If you will demonstrate with a screen reader, test the volume in the room before your turn, and say what it announces as it announces it, because the people at the back will not hear it.

Three things go wrong at this point. The server is not running when your turn comes, because somebody stopped it to run their own; start yours before the session and leave that terminal alone. JavaScript is still off in the browser from your last parity check, so the HTMX path looks broken; check once before you begin. A screenshot that would have carried a link is on a laptop at home; the fallback for every live step is a file already in the repository.

11.3 Activity 2: the crit

Work in groups of five or six. Each person has twelve minutes: five to present, five for questions and answers, two for the group to write. One person keeps time and says "two minutes" and "stop"; the session fails when the first presenter takes twenty. Agree the order at the start so nobody is surprised.

As the presenter, say the four sentences from your outline in order, showing the file or the behaviour behind each. Narrate what is not visible: "I am pressing Tab; focus is on the Edit button; I press Enter". When you have said the fourth sentence, stop, even with time left. Five minutes is enough for one chain and not enough for two.

As a questioner, ask for what you could not see. If the measurement was a rate, ask what the denominator was. If the fix was shown with JavaScript on, ask what the no-JS path does at the same point. If a screenshot stood in for a live step, ask which commit it came from. If you think the fix might have broken something else, ask which regression check covers it. One question each, then a second round if there is time.

As the presenter during questions, answer from the evidence or say that you cannot. "That is rows 41 to 46" is an answer; "I think it was about a third" is not; "I did not record that" is an honest answer you will act on in Activity 4. Do not argue. A question you disagree with is still a question a marker could ask, and the written response is where you say why the evidence does not support the concern.

11.4 Activity 3: the written response

In the last two minutes of each turn, every listener writes three sentences for the presenter: what they saw, what they could not see, and one question they would put to the portfolio. "What they saw" names a link in the chain and the file that carried it. "What they could not see" names a link that was asserted rather than shown. The question is one a reader of the portfolio could reasonably ask. Hand the sheet over or paste the text into the presenter's shared file. Do not put your name on it: it is going into a repository that must contain no names (Chapter 12).

An illustrative example of the three sentences, not a response anyone gave: "I saw the before and after error rates for the edit task and the metrics rows they came from. I did not see the regression check that covers deleting a task now that the edit form has changed. Does the no-JS edit path keep the typed title when validation fails?"

Keep the responses you receive in crit/responses.md, one block per response, as given. Do not tidy or summarise them; the gaps they name are the evidence that you looked for gaps.

11.5 Activity 4: what the presenter records

After your turn, while you still remember the room, write crit/notes.md. For each question you were asked, record the question, what you answered at the time, and what you will do about it. There are three kinds of action. The evidence exists and the portfolio did not point at it: add the link. The evidence does not exist and can be gathered in the time left: gather it, and the note says where it will go. The evidence does not exist and cannot be gathered: write the limitation in a sentence, which goes into the portfolio as something stated rather than something a reader discovers. A question that leads to new work is a new backlog row, with its evidence column pointing at crit/notes.md.

The questions you could not answer are the most useful output of the day. They go to Chapter 12, where each becomes a link, a new file, or a stated limitation.

11.6 What to record

Commit crit/outline.md, crit/responses.md and crit/notes.md. Check that none of the three contains a name, a student number or an email address. Recording the session on video or audio is not part of this chapter; if your group wants to, everyone in the room consents first, and the recording stays out of the repository.
